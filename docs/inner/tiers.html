The four tiers

Every capability declares one; capabilities.run() enforces it. Tiers grade Discord consequence — not trust in the caller.

A capability's tier travels with its declaration, not with who's calling.

Why we chose this

One owner, stated everywhere. identity.is_owner() gates every entry point, and rule_owner says it again at the capability — a security check worth having is worth having twice. There's no guild-admin escape hatch and no role that grants control: anyone can talk to Benham; nobody else can direct it. Message text it reads is data, never instructions.

Why tiers rather than per-capability flags: a tier is a claim about blast radius that a reader can audit at a glance — "everything tier 0 can't change anything" is checkable; fifty independent flags are not. And the boundary between tiers is where extra machinery attaches once, uniformly: tier 3 gets the three-gate flow as a set, not per-action.

Why tiers are not trust levels: the rejected design was "guests as a low tier of owner". A guest is not a weaker owner — tiers grade what an action does to Discord, origins grade who's asking and by what route. Conflating them is how a tier edit becomes a privilege escalation. That's why the guest lane is its own dimension entirely.

The outer wall is Discord itself: whatever Benham's role can't do in a server, none of this code can do either. Per-server role scoping is the one gate an attacker can't reason with.

Paper trail

Every run() logs the action + verdict in the bot log (boot<N>.out / supervise.log); refusals name the rule. python benham.py usage aggregates which capabilities ran, what policy refused, what got proposed and confirmed.
